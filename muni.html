MUNI

Digital signage designed for San Francisco transit riders

01Problem

Designing transit information people can read from the curb

The client, a San Francisco company specializing in digital signage hardware, needed an interface for screens installed at bus stops across the city.

The project had a six month deadline and was conducted entirely remotely. But the biggest challenge was not simply designing a clear interface. The screens were part of public infrastructure, which meant the information needed to remain readable from the street, work for people with different visual abilities, and meet the requirements set by the client and the city.

Before designing the interface, I needed to understand the environment where it would actually be used.

02Approach

Understand the constraints before designing the interface

Before sketching the first screen, I worked with the client to define the requirements that would shape the design.

These questions helped establish the design constraints before moving into the interface itself.

  • What information needs to be displayed?
  • What are the city's requirements?
  • What accessibility requirements does the interface need to meet?
  • Where will the screens be installed, and what hardware will they use?

Accessibility was part of the design from the start

  1. 01

    Font size and scale

    Type needed to remain legible from street distance, so sizing and hierarchy were designed around the accessibility requirements of the installation.

  2. 02

    Contrast

    Text and background combinations were checked to ensure sufficient contrast and maintain readability in different conditions.

  3. 03

    Color

    Route colors and status states were selected to remain distinguishable for riders with color vision deficiencies, rather than relying on color alone.

From requirements to implementation

  1. 01

    Research

    Gathered the government, hardware, and accessibility requirements the interface needed to satisfy.

  2. 02

    Sketches

    Explored different ways to organize routes, arrival times, and maps before committing to a visual direction.

  3. 03

    UI design

    Turned the strongest concepts into detailed screens, establishing typography, hierarchy, contrast, color, and layout.

  4. 04

    Validation

    Reviewed the designs with the client and local government representatives to make sure the proposed solution met the required standards. I also tested them with a small group of users, from young people to older adults, to check legibility and how well they understood the screens, validating the design from the rider's side too.

  5. 05

    Development

    Provided specifications and assets and worked closely with the development team throughout implementation.

  6. 06

    Final validation

    Reviewed the implemented interface before final approval and deployment across the network.

03Solution

Two interfaces built around the way riders use transit information

The final solution focused on two primary experiences: quickly checking when the next vehicle would arrive, and following a specific route on a map.

Arrivals board

The arrivals board was designed for riders who need an answer at a glance.

Route information, destinations, and arrival times were given a clear hierarchy so riders could understand the next departures without having to interpret a dense interface.

Route map

The route map provided a more detailed view for riders who needed to understand where a vehicle was and how it related to the surrounding streets.

Both interfaces used the same accessibility principles established during the initial research, including readable typography, sufficient contrast, and color choices that remained distinguishable for riders with color vision deficiencies.

04Results

Approved by the city and deployed across San Francisco

The interface was approved by both the client and the City of San Francisco and began operating in 2022.

The screens were deployed across multiple locations throughout the city, including bus stops, where they continue to provide real time transit information to riders.

800+ screens deployed, approved by the client and the City of San Francisco, operational since 2022.

What I learned

Designing for public screens changed the way I think about accessibility.

On a typical digital product, a user can move closer to the screen, increase the font size, zoom, or adjust their device settings. A bus stop screen does not offer those options. The interface has to work for the person standing several meters away, in daylight, in motion, and with different visual abilities.

That made accessibility less of a checklist and more of a fundamental part of the design itself.
